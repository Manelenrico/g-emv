## 6. Trust and the advisor that speaks in shapes

**In short.** This section prepares the advisor to enter through the wide door, and it achieves two things. The first is a trust that defends the body: on the bench, an advisor that speaks at random about the others quickly loses its trust, and its good news stops moving the body. Its warnings of danger, on the other hand, are still believed, out of caution. The second is a language for the advisor to propose whole plans, and with it the advisor goes from getting not a single piece of advice through to getting one in ten through. What it does not yet achieve is for the advisor to know the body: it learns to speak the language, but it still does not know well what the body will feel with what it proposes. They have only just met, and in this work the advisor has no way to learn from one game to the next.

**Why trust is needed.** Section 5 ended by saying that what makes the creature's future good goes through the others, and that the part that depends on what they do can only be guessed. If the body is going to listen to someone who guesses, it needs a way of knowing how much to rely on them. And it needs it for a more serious reason: as soon as the body scores what someone promises it, words can move it, and with them errors too, whether unintended or not. Trust is the defense. Here trust is understood as the first bond, the one with oneself, between the body and its head, and it goes in both directions. The body keeps count of how much the advisor's promises resembled what happened afterward; the advisor, of how much notice was taken of it.

**Three test advisors.** Before trying with a real advisor, we tried trust with three made-up advisors, which are not the language model but simple programs, that say things about where the rivals will be at each stop of a plan. The first tells the truth, as far as it can be known: the real position of each rival if either of the two siblings saw it at that moment. The second speaks at random: it gives made-up positions, without looking at anything. The third always says the same thing: that nobody moves. What the advisor says only enters the body's imagination to the extent that it has earned it, with one exception: the bad is always believed whole, because in this body a warning of danger is not discounted.

**Trust defends.** The one that speaks at random loses the body's trust in a few stops and no longer manages to get a single plan through. This is measured without psychology: it is enough to compare what it said with what the body saw afterward.

**But why trust is given matters.** If trust is earned by being right, the one that says nobody moves earns it just as the one that tells the truth does, because in the time a plan lasts the rivals hardly move: being right is easy without knowing anything. If it is earned only by getting right what is new, that someone arrives or that someone leaves, the one that says nobody moves stays at zero and only the one that sees the changes coming earns it. The way trust is handed out decides who is listened to. And there is a detail worth keeping in mind: trusting halfway is the worst. With half trust, the door lets random plans through without giving the good ones enough; the word has to enter whole or not enter at all.

**The real advisor knows no more than the body about the others.** With all that prepared, we went over what the real advisor of the previous work had said in more than 3,000 answers. It talks about the others eight in ten times, but almost always to repeat where they are, which is what it had already been told. When it dares to announce a change, that someone is going to arrive or that someone is going to leave, it is right less often than the assumption that everything stays the same: half the time against almost nine in ten. So trust could not be given to it for its predictions about the others. It is given for what happens after following it: if the body, as it walks the plan, is as it imagined or better, trust goes up; if it is worse, it goes down.

**The language of shapes.** What remained was to give the advisor a way to propose whole plans. It is the most original piece of this part. A plan is expressed as a sentence, and each leg of the plan is a clause with two halves. The world half says where to go and what for: to that corner, for the medkit, next to the sibling. The emotional half says how the body will feel on that leg, in the three dimensions of the model: whether life will go better, the same or worse; whether the hands will fill or empty; whether the bond with the sibling will tighten or loosen; and which row of the table causes it. The grammar of that language is the three dimensions, and its vocabulary, the rows of the table. The body does not believe the emotional half: it imagines each leg with its own table and puts in its own numbers. But that half tells the advisor what it has to think about.

**From zero to one in ten.** We tested it with the same language model as in the previous work, on 200 recorded moments, with the same description of the world and the same instructions, changing only what it was asked for. Asking it for single steps, as in the previous work, the wide door accepted none of 367. Asking it for plans in the language of shapes, one in ten. With a larger model, almost two in ten, but in the competition with the body's options the two models ended up the same. What opens the door is not a larger model: it is the language.

**It speaks the language, but it does not yet know the body.** The advisor learned the grammar right away: almost all its legs had both halves, it used almost all the rows of the table, and it always answered in the format. But what it said the body would feel was seldom right. For the hands it got the sign right two times in ten, less than choosing at random among the three possible answers: it said "the hands will improve" when going toward an object, when in this body's table the lack is only calmed with the weapon already in hand, and the attraction of the ground goes up when getting closer. It gave the sign of desire, not that of need. Where it was right most often was the bond, six in ten, and that is the only dimension about which it was given a real piece of data, what the sibling tells through the thread. What it is given as fact it uses well; what it has to deduce from how the body is made, not yet. It is not that it cannot understand it: it is that nobody has taught it and it has not had time to learn it. In this body there are things that calm down little by little as one gets closer and others that only calm down when one has them in hand, and that cannot be guessed from outside. That is why, for the field, we gave it two aids. We explained to it in one sentence each row that is not intuitive. And in each query we told it how its previous plans had gone: what it said the body would feel, what the body imagined and what it really felt. Whether that was enough for it to learn within a single game is told in section 7.

**Two more things.** Asking it not to guess where the rivals would be worked: with plans, it named them in one answer in seven, whereas with single steps it named them in seven in ten. And it still almost never kept quiet, even though it was given permission: once or twice in a hundred.

**What this establishes.** Trust is a defense that works, as long as it is given for what happens and not for easy predictions. The language of shapes lets the advisor in where the language of steps never did. And the advisor knows how to say where to go, which the body can check, but it does not yet know how to say what the body will feel: it still has to get to know it. With that, everything was ready for the field, which is section 7.
