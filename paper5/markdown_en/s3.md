## 3. A world with time

**In short.** This creature can be given time: a game twice as long, a spell of calm at the start and a ring that arrives late and slowly. With that, the spells without threat multiply by three. But time does not give it a single instant in which it lacks nothing, because what it lacks it carries with it. And with time and without threat, the creature does not start exploring: calm alone does not make it curious.

**Why start with the world.** The earlier work ended by saying that reason had no place in that world because there was no time: life lasted about two minutes, there was always some threat on and each decision was taken in 40 milliseconds. If that was true, before trying reason again the world had to be given time, or everything else would fail again for the same reason.

**The slow world.** We do not change the game; we change what the game lets us configure. A game now lasts twice as long: twelve minutes and forty seconds. At the start there are twenty seconds in which nobody can attack; moving is allowed, but any creature that steps off its starting square dies on the spot. The ring, which in the normal game starts closing early, waits until 40% of the game has gone by and then closes more slowly. The aid packages that fall from the sky, which the game calls sponsor, are the same as in the normal game: each pair gets one pod with two rations for one sibling and a medkit for the other. In the slow world they fall at about a minute and a half and at about three minutes, at 11% and 25% of the game, and they land five seconds after being announced. We call this world the slow world, and it is where everything that follows happens.

**What time gives.** We call calm the instants in which the creature has no fear, its bond with the sibling is at rest and there is nobody armed in sight. In the normal game, calm takes up between three and seven instants out of every hundred. In the slow world, eighteen. Time triples the spells without threat.

**What time does not give.** If we look for the instants in which it lacks absolutely nothing, with no row of its table pulling, there are none. Not one in almost 40,000 instants lived. And what prevents it is not the ring or the rivals: it is what the creature carries with it. Its hands are almost never full: having at the same time a weapon, a medkit and food, which is what its table considers being well supplied, happens in little more than one instant out of every hundred, and almost always in the same lucky creature. Its life is almost never at its maximum, and the life it is missing hurts it even when nobody is touching it. And if the sibling dies, loneliness stays on for the rest of the game. **What it does with the calm.** With time and without threat, one would expect the creature to start looking around. It does not. In the spells of calm it discovers four new squares for every hundred instants; outside calm, fourteen. It sees three times less world when it is calm than when there is danger. It is not that it prefers to stay still: most of that stillness is the legs' pause we talked about in section 2. It is that it has no reason to go and look at what it does not know. Calm does not produce curiosity on its own. This is noted down for section 8.

**Who breaks the calm.** On counting who was landing the hits, something came up that we did not expect. In more than 400 lives from the earlier work, in the first 2,000 instants of the game, a little over a minute, three of the fourteen types of rival landed nine in ten hits and caused eight in ten deaths. The other eleven, in that spell, hardly attacked anyone. One of our creatures was shot dead shortly after setting out, with the initial calm at its maximum: calm is not given by the configuration, it is given by the neighbors. That is why, for the rest of the work, we replaced those three with repeats of rivals that hardly attacked at the start. It is a private world, with chosen neighbors, and we declare it as such. And we also declare an error in that choice, found on reviewing the numbers: we only looked at the first minute of the game, and one of the rivals we repeated is not peaceful, but kills late. Counting whole games, it is the fourth deadliest. And in our own games in the slow world it was the one that killed the most: it caused four in ten of the creature's deaths, none before five and a half minutes into the game, close to halfway. Besides, other rivals that kill early remained, and so did the ring. The comparisons in this work are not affected, because all the creatures faced the same rivals in the same places. But the slow world was more violent than we believed when we designed it: it has fewer hunters at the start, not fewer hunters, and what is measured in it does not say how the creature would fare in a truly quiet world.
