## 1. The question

**Second part of a story.** The first part of this series placed a head that talks beside a body that feels, and asked what it added. The body was the one from three earlier works: an engine with needs on three axes that pull in opposite directions, a table of what hurts, what relieves and what attracts, and a decider that twenty-four times per second imagines a handful of futures, about sixteen, and chooses the one that brings it closest to where it wants to be. The head was a language model that reads the scene and proposes in words. The division of roles has not changed: the advisor proposes and explains; the body imagines, scores and chooses. That first part found that the body heeded the advisor in one proposal out of ten, half of the times to do what it was already going to do, and that the only thing that moved anything was the clock: holding the phrase for four seconds accounted for half of the times the body heeded it.

**What was left open.** That work ended with a suspicion and a debt. The suspicion was in the door, which is what we call the place through which a proposal from the advisor enters to compete with the body's own. That door turned each proposal into a destination one step away and scored it with the same photo the body uses for everything: where it would be on arriving, with the needs of now and the world frozen. Take a piece of advice like "come out of your hiding place now, on the other side there is a medkit and in a while you will be better". That door could not understand it: it only looked at where the body would be on taking the first step, and on that first step leaving the hiding place is worse. The "in a while you will be better" did not fit, because the photo has no "a while later". So when the body did not take a piece of advice, there was no knowing whether it was because the world did not make use of it or because the door did not let it through. The debt was the test that would have separated the two things: passing through the door a piece of advice already known to be good and seeing whether the body recognized it. Without it, there was no knowing whether the instrument measured anything. And a conjecture remained: that the world, a place where some threat was always on, where nobody recognized anybody and life lasted two minutes, did not give time for thinking to be of any use.

**What this work does.** It starts with the three things, in order. First it gives the world time: a slower configuration of the same game, with games twice as long, an initial calm and the late ring, without the three players who attack most at the start. Then it runs the missing test, and the earlier door fails it: it always recognizes what the body already wanted and does not tell a future known to be better from any random square. So another one is built. The wide door lets the advisor propose emotional shapes, which is what we will call a plan with a path and with what the body will feel as it travels it: where the body will go, what it will carry, how it will be in life, in resources and in bonds on each leg. The body does not believe the words; it feels each leg with its own table and decides with a rule that first protects life and then looks at what is gained and what is lost. That door does recognize the good future. With it the advisor is given a language to speak in shapes and a trust that is earned and lost, and it is played in the field. And at the end, with what the field teaches, the body is given a new need, curiosity: to go and see what it does not know. It is a need in the service of survival, because what has not been seen may hide a resource or a danger. And it has an advantage for reason: what the body does not know is something that can be checked, so a plan to go and see it is a plan whose result the body itself can measure.

**What was found, in advance.** Four things were achieved. The first is an instrument: a door that lets a whole plan in and tells good advice from chance, something the earlier one did not do; so as not to fool itself, it only judges the small part of the good that the body can foresee by itself. The second is a finding about the creature: almost everything that improves its future, nine parts in ten, goes through the others: through where they are and what they do with respect to it, and almost half of that is its sibling. That explains why reason helped so little: what matters is exactly what nobody can foresee. The third is a curious creature: the creature that used to spend the calm looking at nothing now proposes trips to itself toward what it does not know, walks them and discovers six times more world while it travels, without getting closer to danger. The fourth, and the most valuable, is commitment: a body able to hold a plan to the end and to let go of it only if something important happens. And what was not achieved is also said: in a hundred games, the advisor's plans gave the body no advantage at all, and overall curiosity only makes it see a little more of the map, because in this world there is almost never calm. Reason still has nothing useful to propose here; what this work leaves is everything it needs to be able to do so in another world.

**Related work.** None of the pieces of this work is new on its own. It is worth saying from whom we take each one and where we depart from them.

The body comes from where it came from in the earlier works. The idea of an agent moved by needs that return to their place, as body temperature returns to thirty-seven degrees, and of emotions that choose behavior without anyone writing it, comes from Cañamero (1997). The hypothesis that a machine that looks after a fragile body ends up having motives of its own comes from Man and Damasio (2019).

Curiosity is not new either. Schmidhuber (1991) proposed giving a machine a reward each time it learns to foresee something it could not foresee before, and Oudeyer and Kaplan (2007) put in order the many ways of doing it that came afterward. Active inference, the theory of Friston and his collaborators, goes further: each step is worth what it gives and what it teaches, and the two things add up in a single sum, so curiosity does not need to be added; it comes out on its own (Friston et al., 2015). And the idea that curiosity is better organized in goals than in loose steps also has a precedent: Santucci, Baldassarre and Mirolli (2016) built a robot that discovers goals by itself and decides which ones to practice according to what it learns from them. From all of them we take the underlying idea: not knowing can be a need, and satisfying it is worth something. We depart in three things. Here curiosity is not a reward added to the rest, but one more need of the body, one that threat switches off. The curious trip, for now, does not serve to learn a skill but to see the world, and for now the world means terrain: squares of the map the body has never seen. It is walked by the same body that flees and eats. And curiosity did not work the first time: as a nudge at each step it was not enough, and it only worked when it became a trip with a destination and commitment (section 8).

Commitment is an idea from philosophy before it is one from engineering. Bratman (1987) held that an intention is a half-finished plan that is not rethought at every moment, and that exactly this makes it useful: whoever revises their plan at every step never gets anywhere. Cohen and Levesque (1990) summed it up in one phrase, intention is choice with commitment, and wrote down when it is reasonable to let it go. Rao and Georgeff (1995) took it into programs that work, the belief, desire and intention agents. From them we take the whole idea: to commit is not to reconsider at every step, and to let go only for a good reason. We depart in where it lives. There, commitment is a rule written in logic. Here it lives in the body: what breaks the trip is what the body feels, a hit, someone armed coming closer, life going down. And here we measure what commitment costs and what it gives.

The advisor that talks follows the line of SayCan (Ahn et al., 2022), already cited in the earlier work: a language model proposes and something that does not talk decides. There, what decides knows what the robot can do; here it knows what the body needs and how it will feel. And this time the model does not propose a step, but a whole plan with the shape of what the body will live through along the way (sections 5 to 7).

We know of no work in which a language model proposes whole plans and those plans have to pass through the consent of a body with needs, which walks them, trusts whoever proposes them according to what they have given it, and lets go of them if they hurt it. That is the proposal. The pieces have owners; the way of putting them together and measuring them is what this work adds.

**How it is told.** First the body, with what is needed to follow the story and two corrections to what the earlier work said. Then the slow world and what time gives and does not give. Then the two doors, the earlier one and the wide one, and what happened when the advisor spoke through the new one: first on the bench, which is what we call the tests on already recorded moments from earlier games, where nothing moves and everything can be repeated, and then in the field, in real games. Then curiosity. At the end, what remains of the question of when thinking is needed, and how everything was measured. The numbers are in the appendix, each with its source.
