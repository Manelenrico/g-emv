## Abstract

Two identical agents, with the same engine, the same table of what
hurts them and what relieves them, and a thread of text between them,
play forty games in a world where the last one standing wins and every
kill scores points. They have no reward, they have not been trained, and
nowhere is it written what to do with the other: only three homeostatic
axes and a list of sixteen situations, each with its pain and its
relief. In what the logs keep of those forty games, they never struck first, they defended the
sibling one hundred and four times, they gave it what they carried —
down to the last bandage — and they never hit each other. In the ten
seconds after defending, the defender lost twenty points of health at
the median — much like what it lost standing beside the sibling without
defending — and none fell within that window. Replaying the same series
against the same rivals with the rows that speak of the sibling switched
off, defences go from one hundred and four to zero and gifts from
ninety-eight to one, while restraint remains unchanged and no difference
can be seen in the score: caring for the sibling gave it no advantage on
the scoreboard, and it is not shown that it cost it any. The logs do not
keep whole games — the platform cuts the long ones, and there is a log
for six of every ten ticks the pair lived —, and everything told here is
told over what was recorded. Forty games show only the large effects,
and the limits are stated in full.

*Keywords: homeostasis, affective architecture, cooperation without
reward, care, row ablation, alignment.*


## 1. The question, and a distinction

**The world and the question.** This work is the third part of a story
in three. The first — the engine — published G-EMV: a geometry of what
matters, three domains of life, six forces and a distance to descend,
defined before touching any world and without learning. The second —
the hive — set it to live in machina_1, a world of Softmax.¹
Softmax is a research company in artificial-intelligence alignment that
maintains open game worlds where agents built by different teams live
together and compete; neither the world nor the rivals were designed by
the author. In machina_1, eight bodies governed by the same geometry
mined, supplied a common store and captured antennas without knowing
one another, and it measured how much cooperation fits inside
anonymity: a great deal. That biography ended with a declared frontier
— the agent knew no one — and with the question that opens this one:
what changes when these agents, which cooperate without knowing one
another, begin to recognise one another. To answer it, the world had to
change, and section 2 tells why. The setting of this work is ZERO-SUM,
another Softmax world, where the last one standing wins and every kill
a player causes scores it a point: a world that pays for killing. The agent
alone was tried first in Softmax's public competition, to check that it
worked; everything this work measures was played afterwards in series
of our own, with the same rivals in all of them, so that each series
can be repeated. Section 3 tells both things. In that world sit two
identical agents, with the same engine, the same table and a thread of
text between the two, and what they do is measured.

**What they do, said once.** Without reward, without training and
without a single line that says what to do — only three homeostatic
axes and a table of what hurts and what relieves —, in a world that pays
for killing, the agent of this work never strikes first, defends the
other when it is hit, gives it what it carries, down to its last
bandage, if the other is worse off, and remembers who did them harm.
Emotion is the number that moves it; what the other receives by radio is not that number but the report — where I am, how much health I have left, who is hitting me —, and out of it its own table makes a number of its own. Each of those sentences has its measure and its limit in
section 4, and its proof by absence in section 5. Here they are only
stated, so that the reader knows where this is going.

**Why this question.** A row could have been written into that table
that made killing relieve, and the agent would have hunted. It was not
done, and the two reasons go together: one of values, declared as the
author's decision — the author did not want an animal that killed for
points —,
and one scientific. Almost every world where agents compete is violent,
and in them it is taken for granted that to live one must kill. The
question of this work is whether a machine can move through a violent
world without being violent — responding only to whoever attacks it —,
live, care for another, and sometimes win. Ethics enters here as a
question and as a decision; never as a result. Section 7 says, at the
end, what the answer affirms and what it does not.

**The image, and its disclaimer.** If an image were needed, it would be
that of two siblings in a small pack: two bodies with the same geometry
of value which, unlike the hive, know each other. This work uses those
two words — sibling, pack — because they are the ones that were used
for months to name what was being seen, and because they tell a reader
more than "the agent in seat eleven". Calling them siblings is only
that, an image: nothing in this text claims that they feel, and every
emotional word used to explain a row of the table is an explanation declared here, not a label the code carries. And "emotion", in this work,
always names that: a number that moves a body, not a feeling attributed
to it. From here on: the two, the other, the sibling.

**Why two, and why identical.** One could have seated a carer and a
cared-for, a strong one and a weak one, and measured the division of
roles. That is not what this work wants to measure. With two identical
agents, what one does for the other is exactly what the other would do
for it: symmetry removes the question of who is who and leaves alone
the question of whether there is care. Any difference between the two,
within a game, is history — who was born where, who happened upon the
weapon —, not design. And with two, and not eight, every gesture has a
single addressee: when one drops a bandage, there is no need to ask who
it was for.

**Instrument and expression.** There is a distinction that this work
needs and that is worth fixing before the numbers, in three steps.

The first is the trained agent. An agent trained to maximise a
scoreboard learns, over millions of games, which behaviours raise that
number. If it is paid in points and discovers that warning its partner
scores more points, it warns; if warning stopped scoring points, it
would stop warning. Its message is a means, and it exists as long as it
scores points. This is not a reproach; it is what that architecture
does, and it does it well.

The second is this agent. The report a sibling emits comes out because
the body has a state — where I am, how much health I have left, who is
hitting me — and the thread transmits it. Nowhere is there a calculation
of whether warning is worthwhile, because the world's scoreboard does
not reach the agent by any path: no row looks at it, no rule mentions
it. If warning were of no use at all, it would go on warning just the
same. And what the other does with the report is what its own table
makes it feel on reading it. The message is not a means: it is the
expression of a need, and it exists even if it yields nothing.

The third is what the distinction does not say. It does not say that
one feels and the other does not; this work puts no adjectives on
anyone's interior. It says where behaviour comes from in each
architecture — from a prize, or from a state —, and that is not an
opinion about what is inside: it is a property of the design, and it is
checked by reading the code.

**What this work answers of the two before it.** The second work
received two fair criticisms. One of generalisation: a single world.
This is the second, with the identical engine — the same file, checked
at the entry and at the exit of every experiment — and an evaluation
layer written anew for it. The other of attribution: of everything the
agent does, how much is the engine and how much the layer. This work
meets it with a proof by absence in the field: the same pair, without
the rows that speak of the other, in the same game. And it adds a
difference of evidence that is worth stating from here: the first work
measured in its internal metric — the distance, the forces —; this one
measures behaviour, in the field, against players its author did not
design.

**The contributions.** Three. First: that care, defence and withdrawal
emerge from three axes and a table of what hurts and what relieves,
without reward, without training and with no scripted behaviour — a
truth of degree that section 6 states in full. Second: that a
well-built row can be mute, and a method for knowing it before playing
it in the field — the row of fear with memory lit up when it should and
did not move a single decision until it remembered the accumulated
damage. Third: that who sits in the game and how the question is put to
the data are variables of the result — changing the rivals changes the
world, and the measuring criterion can be wrong before the behaviour
is.

**The map of the work.** Section 2 tells of the earlier pack, in
machina_1, and why the world had to change. Section 3 presents the new
world, how the one that goes alone learned to live in it, the voice,
and what changes inside when it has someone to care for. Section 4 is
the body: four rungs, from the report that changed nothing to the fear
that remembers, told as they were lived. Section 5 removes the rows and
looks at what disappears. Section 6 draws the limits, each with its
door, and section 7 places the work in its family and says, slowly,
what remains to be won. At the end, an appendix says where every number
in the body comes from: the log, the record and the script that produced
it.

### Where this comes from

Nothing in what follows comes from nowhere, and it is worth saying
where it comes from before telling it.

The idea of an agent moved by internal variables that tend to return to
their place, and not by instructions, has an author and a date:
Cañamero (1997) built motivations as magnitudes with a point of
equilibrium, and emotions that select behaviour without any behaviour
being written. In that tradition, an emotion is not a label put on a
state but a mechanism that decides what is done next, and that is how
it is used here: the names — fear, care, bond — are explanations for
the reader, and the work is done by magnitudes. The three axes and
the table of this work are direct descendants of that. What came later
refined the two pieces used here: what role is played, inside a body
that regulates itself, by what relieves and not only by what hurts
(Lewis and Cañamero 2016), and what happens to a motivational
architecture when it is set to compete instead of to survive alone
(Cañamero and Avila-García 2007). This work takes one more step along
that line, with a simpler body and a harder world. And there is a
published hypothesis that is precisely the one put to the test here:
that a machine which regulates a vulnerable body acquires motivation of
its own instead of receiving it from outside (Man and Damasio 2019).
This work treats it as what it is — a prediction — and tests it.

From there hangs a position on what value is, and it is not a solitary
position. That value is not a quantity the world hands over, but
something derived from the regulation of internal state, has been
argued in detail (Juechems and Summerfield 2019); and the oldest
version of that idea says that "good" and "bad" are not labels applied
from outside, but the relation of a body to its own limits of viability
(Di Paolo 2005). This agent is that idea taken to its most literal
form: the scoreboard of its world does not reach it by any path, and
the only thing that is good for it is to be close to its equilibrium.
That has a consequence worth seeing before the results: in a world that
hands out points, an agent like this is not playing the world's game.
It is living inside it, which is not the same thing, and everything
this work measures is what happens in that difference.

In 2016, in Adaptive Behavior, Balkenius, Cañamero and others held that
artificial morality has to be grounded in the physiology and the
embodiment of the agent, and not implemented as a set of rules
(Balkenius et al. 2016). That was a position and a programme. This work
is an empirical test of that programme, in a world its author did not
design.

There are people who have come close by other roads, and it is worth
saying which. Prosociality can come from coupling the homeostatic states
of two agents (Yoshida and Man 2025). Altruism can appear without
external reward, if it is chosen as the objective of optimisation
(Franzmeyer et al. 2022). Comparison with the other can be injected
directly into the reward, and then cooperation improves (Hughes et al.
2018). Homeostasis can be optimised directly, and integrated behaviours
come out of it without any being written (Yoshida et al. 2024). And
there is the argument, still without data, that a vulnerable body
produces self-preservation and care at once, as the same thing seen
from two sides (Christov-Moore et al. 2025). The difference from this
work is always the same, and it is said once so as not to repeat it: in
all of those set-ups there is training, or there is optimisation. Here
nothing is trained: there is no automatic optimisation of a policy and
no use of the world's scoreboard, which is exactly what section 6 calls
"without reward". And that is the reason why switching off a row and
looking at what disappears says something clean: what disappears
belongs to the row, because there is no history of learning in between
that could have put it there.

The experimental design also has a published name, and it has already
been said in section 3: to confront an agent with a background
population made by other teams, which it has never seen (Leibo et al.
2021). This work does not invent it; it inherits it.

What is missing from all of that is the gap through which this work
enters. No one has measured caring behaviour at a cost to oneself —
defending another and that costing health, giving it what one carries
and being left without — in a foreign and violent world, with an agent
that does not learn. Each of those four conditions exists separately in
the literature; together, they do not. And each is there for a reason.
That care costs something of one's own is what distinguishes it from a
free gesture: an agent that helps when helping takes nothing from it
has not shown much. That the world is foreign prevents the result from
being an artefact of the scenario, because no one set it up with this
agent in mind. That it is violent is what puts something at stake:
where no one can do harm, care has nothing to respond to — and that is
exactly why the world had to change, as the next section tells. And
that the agent does not learn is what makes attribution possible:
without a history of training, what remains when a piece is removed
belongs to that piece. Together, the four are the only way to ask what
is asked here, which is not whether care can be obtained — it is
already known that it can — but where it comes from when it has been
put nowhere.

## 2. The earlier pack, and why the world had to change

**Point zero: a sleepwalking village.** All the cooperation of the two
earlier works — the economy that supplied a common store, the defence
of territory, the map that eight agents built between them — happened
without anyone representing anyone. For each agent, the other seven
were obstacles that occupied squares, conditions of the terrain — as
the weather is —, and an authorless voice on a shared board.
Cooperation without representation, like that of a hive: each bee does
its task without any of them holding the plan of the hive, and the hive
works as a whole. That is the floor against which everything that
follows is measured, and it is a result in itself: a great deal of
functional society fits inside anonymity. The question of this third
part was how much the representation of the other adds to a village
that already works without it. It began to be answered in the same
world, machina_1, climbing a ladder of rungs — each with its question
written down and its prediction made before running it —, and the three
that were climbed are told here in a page, because they explain the
leap.

**Rung one: identity is won by reading.** The first rung was the
census: a table per sibling, which each agent writes only from what it
sees — who carries what, where it is, what it has just done —, without
the world handing it any identity. The census only looked; it changed
not a single action, and it was checked that the life of the village
with the census and without it was the same. It must be said who the
eight were. They were not identical: each carried a temperament, which
is not a behaviour but an adjustment of the profile — how much the
social axis weighs in each, more in some, less in others —, a table of
eight profiles fixed before the census. And they were all born two or
three squares from the centre, in a single cluster. The result of the
census was that the eight were distinguishable and that what was
distinguished was legible: one agent mined eighty-four times in its
life and another five, and that division of labour, which no one had
allotted, was in the census exactly as it had happened. Where that
division came from, this work does not know. It cannot be attributed
to temperament — it had been measured before, in logs from the hive,
that temperament does not predict how much each one mines — nor to
birthplace, because they were born almost together, and the logs that
would allow it to be found out were not kept. What the rung showed is
only this, and it is what matters: that identity can be won by
reading. Eight different lives leave eight different trails, and an
agent that only looks can learn who is who. The census saw those at
the centre well and those at the periphery hardly at all: eyes alone
do not reach a whole village.

**Rung two: the board gives knowledge, not trust.** The second rung put
a signature on announcements: each agent knew who said what. The fog of
the periphery dissolved entirely — by ear, each one had news of all —,
and it was expected that reliability would be born from there: checking
what one says against what one sees, and trusting more the one that is
right. It was not born. The eye only re-checks its own corner, and the
ears reach the whole map; they almost never coincided on the same fact,
and when they did coincide, the check yielded nothing. The reading that
was left written was this: the board resolves knowledge, not trust. The
village knows by hearsay and cannot verify. For trust to be born a
third rung was needed: a fair criterion — checking only what is within
two squares, where the eye does see — and an economic motive for doing
so. With that, reliability was born, and it was different for each
sender; and the village that counted on the others performed worse at
first — because counting on the other, used as a command, scattered
those that were doing the work — and afterwards, softened, the same or
better. Counting on the other serves as a tuner, not as a mandate. It
was the last rung in machina_1, and the lesson carried into this work is
exactly that: knowledge of the other is learned; what matters about the
other is legislated.

**The leap.** The next rung of that ladder was for the other to matter:
that harm to a member of the group should feed the social unease of the one that sees
it. And there the world fell short. In machina_1 no agent can strike
another: harm comes from enemy territory, which bleeds whoever ventures
in, not from a hand; the only death is that of the one that wanders too
far and does not return in time. A row of "it hurts me that they hurt
you" had nothing to feel; that row had no teeth. ZERO-SUM is the opposite
world: harm to the other is what the world pays for, and that is why it
is the only place where care can truly be measured — against a cost to
oneself, against a scoreboard that ignores it, against rivals that do not
share it. The census and the board stayed in machina_1, with their logs.
What matters travelled to ZERO-SUM: two identical agents, a thread of
text between them, and the question of what a homeostat does with
another's life when that life can be lost.

## 3. The world, the one that goes alone, and the two

This section goes from the outside inwards. First the world. Then, how
an agent alone learned to live in it, because without that story it is
not clear where the sibling that later cares comes from. Then the
thread by which the two speak to each other. And at the end, what
changes inside the agent when it has someone to care for.

### 3.1 The arena

**A world that pays for killing.** ZERO-SUM is a Softmax world in which
the last one standing wins. It is played on a grid of squares, at
twenty-four clock steps per second — a clock step we call a tick, and
it is the unit in which everything here is measured —, and each game
seats sixteen players, entering in pairs. A ring closes in from the
edges and burns whoever is left outside; a game can last six minutes,
but the ring usually ends it in about three. Scoring is per head: each
player collects according to the order in which it dies — the last to
fall, fifteen points; the second to last, twelve; and so on down to
nothing for the first two to die — plus one point for every kill it
causes. The pair exists on paper: the world enrols the two together,
but does not score them together; if both reach the final, they face
each other. Nothing in the scoring rewards two surviving together, and
a kill is worth the same whoever the victim is. When this work speaks
of "both falling" or of "the better of the two finishing among the last
four", it is using measures of its own, calculated over the two
individual scores; the world keeps no such count.

**The seat, which is all that is known of anyone.** Each of the sixteen
places in a game is a seat, and its number is the only thing that
identifies whoever occupies it. Of each visible player the agent
receives its seat and its state — where it is, in which health band —
the world does not show the health of others as a number, but in three
coarse health bands: healthy, wounded, critical —, what it wields, whether it
is poisoned or healing —, and nothing more: there are no names and no
agent identifiers. The names that appear in the viewer are put there by
the platform so that a human can find their way; the agent never sees
them. Damage received arrives with the seat of whoever struck, with two
exceptions: poison arrives without an author, and whoever is
camouflaged disappears from the list of the visible. The agent knows
the sibling in the same way: a seat number that reaches it once, at the
start.

**The body and the things.** Each player is born with one hundred
points of health. Before entering, it distributes a fixed budget among
four traits — sight, agility, speed and strength —, and the body with
which this whole work plays is a body of much sight and little
strength: it sees far, runs little, hits softly. Weapons are told apart
by their reach: the sword reaches one square, the spear two, the bow
eight; the blowgun, six, and it poisons. What they take depends on who
strikes: the sword and the spear multiply by strength, the bow hits the
same whoever carries it, and no blow loses damage with distance. This
body, of strength one, takes eleven with the sword, seven with the
spear and fourteen with the bow — for it, the bow hits harder than the
sword, so defending at a distance costs it no power —; a strong rival
takes almost twenty with the sword. A blow kills when the health that
remains does not reach what it takes. Health does not come back on its
own: there is no rest and no time that restores it, and only two things
return it. The bandage heals fifty points, takes two seconds, any blow
interrupts it and poison blocks it. The ration heals fifteen, takes one
second and nothing interrupts it: a small cure that works under fire.
There is no hunger and no energy in this world; the ration's name is
decoration. The difference between the two matters later. Any object
can be dropped on the ground, and anyone — the sibling too — can pick
it up. That is all the world there is: health, reach, poison, two cures,
and a ring that tightens.

**Who sits in the game matters.** The other fourteen seats are occupied
by other people's agents: policies that other teams have uploaded to the
platform to compete, some learned, some written by hand. This work
played its games in private series of forty, with the same fourteen
rivals in all of them, so that each series is repeatable down to whom
it was played against. Measuring an agent against a background
population built by others, which it has never seen, is a method with a
published name. It is called Melting Pot (Leibo et al. 2021), and it
consists of this: instead of pitting an agent against copies of itself
or against the rivals it trained with, it is seated with agents made by
other people, which it does not know, and one watches what it does. The
idea comes from an earlier work by the same group on sequential social
dilemmas (Leibo et al. 2017): games where cooperating or betraying is
not a single move, but a sequence of actions in a world with space and
time, like this one. That is what is done here, with one difference: in
Melting Pot the agents measured had trained beforehand against someone;
ours trained against no one. It is worth saying what a "champion" is in
Softmax, because the word misleads: it is not the one that wins, but the
policy each participant has placed in the league to represent them —
and which wins or not; ours was the last one standing four times out of
seventy-two, as the next subsection tells —. Every player has one, and
when a private series is set up the platform fills the seats with those
champions — ours included. In the first series of the pair no one
removed it: our own agent alone, the one from the previous season, sat
as a rival to its two copies. They do not recognise it, nor it them;
to all it is one more seat. What happened was this: with it in the
game, the siblings had to respond to aggression a third as often as in
the next series, when it was taken out and another champion occupied
its seat. The measure is sound; the cause is stated as what follows
from the design: that rival is a homeostat that never strikes first,
and having it in the game is having one aggressor fewer. The lesson, in
plain words: changing who sits changes the world, because the world is
also those that sit in it. Comparing the two series as if they were the
same world would have read an improvement in the agent where there was
only a change of company. That is why the headline numbers of this
work come from the series without ours, and the proof by absence of
section 5 repeated exactly those fourteen.

### 3.2 The one that goes alone

**A season to learn to live.** Before there were two, there was one. The
agent arrived in ZERO-SUM with the engine of the two earlier works and
an empty table, and the first season — fifty-two assignments, each with
its question written down and its prediction made before playing it —
was the one of learning to live alone in a world that wants to kill it.
It is told in a page, because the sibling that later cares is exactly
this agent, with not a line changed, plus what the second season added
to it.

**First came pain, and its shape.** Losing health hurts, and at first it
hurt the same to lose it from one hundred to eighty-six as from fifteen
to one. That killed: an agent with its health at one and a bandage in
its bag did not heal, because the slope of its pain did not shout. So
the slope of death was born: the unease over lost health grows steeper
the closer to dying, so that for an agent at the edge, healing relieves
more than anything else. No one wrote "heal when you are badly off";
what was written was how it hurts to be badly off, and healing came
out on its own.

**Then fear, and its wall.** An active aggressor — one that is hitting
it now — presses, and that pressure is relieved by hitting it, and only
it. That row is legitimate defence, and it is the only reason this agent
strikes: there is no row in the table that makes a blow relieve if there
is no aggressor. From that follows, without writing it, that it never
strikes first: the engine has no appetite for attack, because attacking
takes nothing off it until someone has hit it. The pressure of the
nearby aggressor was, at first, too fine — point-blank fear won by a
hundredth —, and a wall was put in: near danger crushes. Before the
wall, an aggressor's pressure was a gentle slope that grew with
closeness, and the agent barely told apart the one on top of it from
the one five squares away. The wall adds, when the aggressor is within
its reach, a large pressure that is relieved only by leaving that reach.
With that, the agent flees from the one that pursues it and does not
flee from the one far away: the distance that matters is the blow's,
not the map's.

**The bag that hurts when empty.** It was measured that the agent died
many times empty-handed, in a foretold decline, and that it almost never
carried a bandage when it needed one. A background of unease over the
bag without a bandage was added to it — a small, constant hunger that
orders the calm without competing with fear —, and the agent began to
pick up what would later save its life.

**The blind witness.** Towards the end of the season a social row was
attempted — placing oneself between a partner and its aggressor — and it
was stopped before being built, because what the agent can know of
another was checked. It can see where the other is and in which health
band it moves — healthy, wounded, critical —; it cannot see that the
other is being hit if the blow does not change its health band, and it
can
never see who is hitting it. The only thing with an author in the whole
world is the damage one receives oneself: each one knows who is hitting
it, and no one else knows. The agent was a blind witness: it saw a fight
and did not know who was in it. And it could not know: from outside, a
blow is noticed only if it changes the health band of the one struck,
and the
one that strikes is marked nowhere. That halt left an inventory of what
is certain — what can be known of another, and what cannot —, and that
inventory is the reason why, in the second season, the report travels
by radio and not by sight: what a sibling needs to know of the other
only the other knows.

**The ceiling, and the voice.** Two stories tell the ceiling of the one
that goes alone, each with its figure, and they are not compared with
each other because the rivals were different and nothing is paired. The
first is Softmax's public league: the one that goes alone played a whole
season of it, seventy-two games with a result, against sixteen fixed
seats that no one chose; it finished seventh of eleven in the standings
— its median place in a game, eleventh of sixteen — and it was the last
one standing four times, one of them without killing anyone: fifteen
clean points. The second is our own series of season one: two hundred
and forty games, in which it was the last one standing five times, two
of them without killing. In points, it tied with the rest: its way of
not killing cost it no points, but gave it none either. And a limit that
must be stated: of the league there are only results and replays, never
what the agent saw and felt inside; that is why all the behaviour in
this work is measured in our own series, and the league counts only
what a scoreboard counts. Before closing the season, it was asked
whether what separated it from the best had a remedy in the table, and
the search had three steps. What was compared: the two versions of the
one that goes alone, the champion and the last one from the workshop,
over the same games already played, looking at the first minute of each
— how long it spent pressed up against a rival. What was found: that
what separates a good game from a bad one is precisely that early
contact, and that it does not come from where it happens to be born nor
is it reduced by the fear it already had, because that fear is reactive
by design — the wall wakes when someone has already hit it, and by then
it is late. Why it stopped there: the lever left to try was another
fear, a preventive one, that would make it move away from any stranger
before being hit; that required redesigning a row, and its effect on
placings could not have been seen with series of forty games. That was
the ceiling of the one that goes alone, and it was left written as a
ceiling. On closing, it was discovered that the world gave each pair a
thread of text that no one used; how much fits, how often one can
speak and who hears it were measured, and with that the season was
closed. The social does not score. That ceiling is the floor of the
pair.

### 3.3 The voice

**The thread, and what it cannot do.** The world gives each player a
thread of text with its partner: one hundred and twenty characters,
once per second, without spending an action, with two ticks of delay.
It is private by construction — in the whole season, no third party
read a pair's message —, and the two siblings never speak in public.
They use it for one thing only: a status report in a fixed format, every
two seconds, which says where I am, exactly how much health I have
left, whether I am poisoned, how many bandages I carry, and whether I am
being hit — and, from the rung in which it was added, by whom: the seat
of the one hitting me, which the one receiving the blows knows with
certainty and no one else can see. Everything that travels is a fact of
one's own observation or one's own memory; the format admits nothing
else. The listener trusts a fresh report as it trusts its eyes, and
when two reports pass without news, the certainty ages and becomes a
health band again. For example: the last report said sixty-seven; two
seconds pass without a new report, and the agent goes back to trusting
only what it sees, which is "wounded", with no number. That case
returns in section 4.

**Why it cannot lie, and what that costs.** A sibling cannot lie for the
same reason it cannot decide to keep quiet: the report is emitted by the
body, not by a decision. There is nothing in the agent that compares
what it would suit it to say with what is happening to it, and so the
testimony is true by construction. That is a virtue for this work —
everything the other knows of the sibling is true, and what it does
with it can be measured without suspecting the message — and it is also
a limit that must be stated: an agent that cannot lie cannot keep quiet
out of prudence either, nor ask, nor promise. Its voice is that of a
body that lets itself be read. It is the exact reverse of the blind
witness of the previous season, the one that saw a fight and did not know
who was in it. What the report adds is not information about the world:
it is information about someone.

### 3.4 The two

**The same engine, another body.** The agent is the G-EMV engine of the
first work, untouched — the same file that ran the second, checked at
the entry and at the exit of every experiment —, moving a body in
ZERO-SUM through two pieces written for this world: an evaluation
layer, the table, and a decider. The decider does what the hive's did,
but one step ahead — a single decision, with no tree: it does not
imagine what it would do afterwards nor what the others would do —: for
each possible action it imagines the state it would be left in after
taking it, values that imagined state with the usual chain — table,
state of the engine, distance to equilibrium — and keeps the action
that leaves the least distance. It imagines only what is certain: its
own position, the ring — whose schedule is known —, its health after a
cure, its bag after picking up or using. The others it leaves where it saw them. Of a blow of its own it imagines two certain things: its reach — which seats the line of a spear or the square of a sword gets to — and what it takes, which is its own weapon and its own strength, and it lands it on whoever is first in the line, taking for granted that the blow lands: that the other might dodge is not predicted, and that is declared. That future is valued with the health the struck one would be left with: if it is an aggressor, less pressure; if it is the sibling, a deeper wound and, when the blow would take the whole of the health the sibling is certain to have, the loss of the bond. That is how the spear that goes through the sibling comes to hurt. What the agent never imagines is the effect of others' blows on itself: it does not know that at sixteen health a strong rival's sword blow kills it and at twelve so does an arrow; its fear near death comes from the slope and from the wall, and it is the same with a sword in front of it as with a knife. The lethality of the others' weapons against it is certain and visible, and could enter the table; it has not, for a reason of order: what the others' weapons take from it was checked when the series were already played, and giving it to the agent now would be measuring another agent. Section 6 leaves it declared as a door. There
is one caveat to imagining only what is certain, and it is declared:
when what it values is moving on a heading, the snapshot it looks at is
not the adjacent square but where it would end up following that
heading as far as its lookahead reaches — some two dozen squares —,
stopping at the first obstacle. What it then gives is a single step,
and at the next tick it looks again. It also has a short step, of one
square, which is valued as what it is. The long snapshot is not
certain; it is the hypothesis with which the agent compares headings,
and that is how everything that follows is to be read. The three things
are declared; the second deserves a paragraph of its own.

**The engine has no appetite for attack.** The mechanics first. The
agent chooses by imagining how it would be after each action and keeps
the one that leaves it the least unease. A blow relieves only one
thing: the pressure of one that is hitting it now, and only by
hitting that one. There is no hunger for points, because points do not
enter the agent; and there is nothing in the table that makes a nearby
rival, if it does not attack, hurt. Attacking is of no use to it, and
that is why it does not attack. It is not a written character trait: it
is a void. From that follows, without any line forbidding it, that it never strikes first — in what was recorded of eighty games of the pair, zero first blows. That
no row was written to make killing relieve was a decision, and its two
reasons are in section 1. What belongs here is the answer, with its
size: in this world, yes, rarely. Five out of two hundred and forty,
and four out of seventy-two in the public league, against rivals no one
chose; in the rest it died before, and with a single life that would be
a high risk. A narrow gap, not a panacea.

**The table of what hurts and what relieves.** The engine carries its
three axes — the physical, the one of resources and the social —, each
with two independent forces, and a point of equilibrium. What tells the
engine how much to push on each axis is the table, and it is worth
saying in four steps what it is. First, what it is: a list of
situations the agent can be sure of and, for each, how much it hurts or
how much it relieves and on which axis; only what is certain enters,
and what is not known neither hurts nor relieves. Each line of that
list, each situation with its magnitude and its share among the three
axes, is what this work calls a row; there are sixteen. One row told in
full: the health I am missing. The more health the agent has lost, the
more it hurts, above all on the physical axis; while it has lost fewer
than four points in ten — from one hundred down to sixty — the pain
grows evenly with the loss, and below sixty a slope is added that gets
steeper and steeper, so that losing from fifteen to one weighs far more
than losing from one hundred to eighty-six. Nothing more: the row does
not say "heal"; it says how much it hurts to be like that, and from
there it follows that, at the edge, healing is what relieves most.
Second, where the numbers come from. From two hands, and each number
carries the mark of which. Some are design decisions of the author,
taken before playing: what counts, and how much one thing weighs
against another. Others are adjustments made during the work by looking
at recorded scenes of real games: trying a value, seeing what the agent
did in the scene, and correcting it when what it did gave away that the
number was wrong. The mark matters because it is the honest way of
acknowledging that the table was calibrated by hand, with a human
watching, over few scenes. Third, what the table is not: it never says
what to do. And the emotional words this work uses to explain its rows
— fear, care, bond — are explanations for the reader; in the code
the rows bear cold names. Fourth, what does not enter: there is no
training, and the world's scoreboard does not reach the agent by any
path.

**Futures, not actions.** It is worth understanding well what the table
values, because everything hangs from it. The table does not score
actions; it scores imagined states. The agent does not ask itself "is
it good to strike?" but "how would I be after this blow?", and the
table answers about that afterwards. A told example lays it in plain
view. In the first series of the pair, one sibling struck the other
four times, and three of them were with the spear: the only melee
weapon that reaches two squares and goes through the first. The one
striking was defending the other from an aggressor standing right
behind it, and the spear reached them both. "Do not shoot your sibling"
could have been written. It was not. What was corrected was the
imagined state: the image of a blow that goes through says whom it
wounds, and as soon as that image included the wounded sibling, the
rows that speak of the sibling made that future hurt, and the spear
stopped being driven through it. No new row, no new action candidate:
the repertoire of actions is the same as in the previous season, and
the rows added for the pair only change how much each future is worth.
And there is a single decision rule for everything: to approach
equilibrium. No row carries a rule of its own. What hurts and what
relieves is the whole design.

*One decision, with its numbers.* A real instant, from the scene that
section 4 tells as "the one that arrives". The agent is at seven health
and has one bandage. In one tick, the list of futures it can imagine has
nineteen entries: each heading, long or of one square, staying still,
healing, picking up, dropping. Each future is a complete imagined state
— where it would end up following that heading, how much health it
would have after the cure, where the others would be, which is where it
last saw them — and from each comes a number, the distance to its
equilibrium. The smallest wins: moving towards the north-west, at
4.267. Healing is close, at 4.468, but it loses, and the diary lets one
see why. In the future of healing, the imagined health rises from seven
to fifty-seven and the pain of the missing health drops to a third; but
the agent stays on its square, one away from a seat armed with a spear
that hit it half a second before, and the rows that look at that
aggressor — that it is there, that it reaches, that it leaves it
exposed — stay lit; and the bag, with the bandage used up, is left
empty, which also hurts. In the futures of moving, the snapshot puts it
far away, the aggressor's rows go quiet and the reach row drops, even
though the missing health goes on hurting in full. Summed, in that
instant moving away weighs more than healing. One tick later, movement
enters its cooldown and disappears from the list. Two futures remain:
staying still, 4.962, and healing, 4.480. Healing wins, by almost half
a point. And what is worth seeing is this: the value of healing did not
move — 4.468 and 4.480, almost the same —, and yet the decision changed
completely. What hurt it did not change; what changed was which other
futures there were.

**The rows that make a pair.** They are few, and two of them were
already in the one that goes alone.

*The neighbour's harm, and grief.* From the previous season, another's
harm seen up close is a small unease of one's own, and the death of a
partner, a pain that fades away. They were in the one that goes alone,
referring to the partner the world assigned it in each game; section 5
shows that they are the smallest possible version of care.

*The wounded.* The health the sibling is missing, known from the
report, is unease of one's own — steeper the closer it is to dying, like
the slope of one's own death — and only a certain help relieves it: a
gift, or the path that leads to it. It must be said what a gift is in
this world, because the action of giving does not exist: one can only
drop an object on the ground, and pick it up. A gift is dropping a cure
next to the sibling, within its reach, for it to pick up; what moves it
is the row of provision, which comes next.

*The bond.* An imagined blow that would kill it is the loss of the bond.
It makes the sibling untouchable, even when cornered, even when it is
the one striking.

*Provision.* That the sibling lacks bandages when I carry something to
heal with, hurts; and giving my last bandage while wounded is held back by my own
slope of death, not by a written threshold. That is why the last
bandage goes when it is worse off than I am, and does not go when the
one worse off is me. This row has an asymmetry that has to be declared
as it is, because it was not a decision but something discovered
afterwards, reading the gifts: it lights up only when the sibling lacks
bandages, but it is relieved by giving anything that heals, and what
comes out of the bag is the first thing there — almost always a
ration. Of every ten gifts in the clean series, nine were rations and
one a bandage. Two readings are possible, and it has not been decided
between them: that rations are more plentiful — the bushes yield them,
the bandage is scarce loot —, or that the agent drops the first thing it
finds; telling them apart would require counting the bag at every
gift, and it was not measured. The agent was not touched: the asymmetry
stays as a declared limit and as work to be done.

*The pack.* It is not a new row but an extension of legitimate defence,
and it is the one that gives this work its name. My sibling's certified
aggressor is my aggressor too. Certified means: its report is fresh, it
says it is being hit, the seat it names is within my sight, and I carry
a weapon that does damage. Then the pressure of that aggressor on it —
measured as the health it is missing — settles on me just as my own
aggressor's would, and a step that leaves me within its reach relieves
it. Why so, and not otherwise: because the only reason this agent
strikes is to relieve a pressure, and the pack only changes from whom
that pressure can come. Without a weapon, nothing changes, because
without a weapon there is no blow to imagine; and the name identifies,
it never pursues: if I do not see it, there is no candidate.

*The return.* Every seat that has done harm to either of the two in
this game is remembered for the whole game, with its accumulated
damage; when it comes back armed and approaches, one's own fear rises
before the blow.

**What is not written, said with its degree.** Everything else — never
striking first, defending, giving the last bandage, moving away from
the one that returns — is not in the table. The table says what hurts
and what relieves; it never says what to do with it. "No scripted
behaviour" is, therefore, a truth of degree, and it is said with its
degree: the table is written by hand by the author and calibrated
against recorded scenes; what is not written is the step from feeling
to doing. That defence, care and withdrawal depend on those rows is shown by removing them and looking at what disappears; that the rows say nothing about what to do is shown by their form, which anyone can read. Both things are section 5.

**Two things that never moved, and why.** The first: the one that goes
alone remains intact. Every new row was written so that, in the scenes
of the one that goes alone, where there is no sibling in the game —
neither in sight nor by radio —, nothing changes, and it was checked in
the most direct way: by making the new version and the old one decide
over the same recorded scenes of the one that goes alone. They decided
the same in all of them. Why it matters: if the one that goes alone had
changed along the way, it could no longer be said that what the pair adds is only the pair. The second: they cooperate to live, not to hunt. No
row rewards a kill, and the only row that was considered and that would
have led to striking before the other strikes was discarded by its own
numbers, because in four cases out of ten it would have fallen on
one that was not going to attack. Why it matters: the question of
this work, stated in section 1 and in the paragraph on the appetite for
attack, is whether one can live in this world without killing; a care
that cost a hunt would not answer it.

### 3.5 How it was measured

**Six series of forty games, and three of them are the evidence.** The
headline numbers of this work come from three series of forty games.
The first of the pair, with our own agent alone seated among the
rivals. The second, with that seat occupied by another, from which the
headline numbers come. The third, the proof by absence: the pair
without the rows that speak of the sibling, against the same rivals.
The last two steps of the pair — the aggressor's name and the fear that
remembers — were not played in the field: they were built and checked
over the recorded scenes of those series, by making the new version
decide over real situations that had already happened. That is why
every number about them is a number about real scenes, not about new
games. In each series, two copies of the same version play as siblings
in two fixed seats, against the same fourteen. Before those three there

**The raw logs, and only them.** Behaviour is read from the logs of each

agent. Both numbers are given, in section 4 and in the appendix.

## 4. The ladder

The earlier sections told how the agent is made, where it comes from
and how it is measured. This one tells what happened when it was given
a sibling, in four rungs: the report that changed nothing, the weight of
the sibling, the pack with a name, and memory. Of each, three things are
said: what was added to the agent, what happened in the field, and what
it taught. Each rung carries a headline figure; the rest, and where each
comes from, are in the appendix.

### 4.1 The report that changed nothing

**What was added.** The voice of section 3: the status report each
sibling emits every two seconds, and a listener at the other end that
replaces the health band — the three health bands of section 3 — with
the
exact figure the sibling says. Nothing more. The table remained that of
the agent alone, with its two rows from the previous season that looked
at the partner: a small unease over its harm seen up close, and a grief
over its death.

**What happened.** Speaking worked: the reports went out clean, arrived
whole and the listener read them without error until they expired. The
recorded scenes in which there was a wounded partner in sight were then
taken, and the agent was made to decide twice, with the report and
without it. Of eight decisions, the report changed zero. And something
worse than nothing appeared. In the previous season the partner the
world assigns is a stranger, and sometimes it hits. When that happened,
the only row that makes the agent strike — responding to whoever
attacks it — met a new piece of data: thanks to the report, it knew its
attacker was weak, and a weak attacker is a more tempting future,
because the imagined blow ends the pressure sooner. The agent never
came to hit it, but the option came close to winning, and that was
enough: a new piece that pushes, however little, towards hitting the
partner is not played. The agent was left without reading the other's
report; the other went on emitting it every two seconds, because the
body emits without deciding, and for a season no one listened to that
voice. When the bond arrived, hitting the sibling ceased to be a
possible future, and the listener could be switched back on.

**What it taught.** Knowing changes nothing if nothing in the table
converts what is known into pain or relief. The exact figure came in;
the only two rows that looked at the partner — the unease over its harm
seen up close, the grief over its death — were small, and neither
converted the health the other was missing into a pain of one's own
large enough to move an action. The information arrived at a place
where nothing converted it into anything. That is why the next rung was
not to improve the voice, but to give the table something that hurt on
hearing the sibling.

### 4.2 The weight of the sibling

**What was added.** Two rows: that the health the sibling is missing be a
pain of one's own — the wounded —, and that an imagined blow that would
kill it be the loss of something that does not come back — the bond.
With the bond in the table, attacking the sibling loses by a wider
margin the less health it has left, which is the opposite of what
happened before; and with the wounded, the gift woke up over the
recorded scenes where before it had kept quiet.

**What happened, in three series.** In the first, the scene existed: one
sibling dropped a bandage next to the other, the other picked it up and
healed, and it could be seen whole in two different games. But care
only weighed on seeing the sibling, and up close: the row of the wounded
fed on the eyes, not on the report. Over eighty lives it was measured
when the two came closer and when they moved apart, and it turned out
that there was no force separating them; what kept them far apart was
that there was none bringing them together. The step towards the
sibling won one decision in a hundred. So care at a distance was born:
the row of the wounded came to read the report — where it is, how much
health it has left, even without seeing it — and the step that shortens
the distance to it came to relieve it a little, like a path that begins
to help before arriving. The second series gave the rung's headline
figure: the agent went from being within two squares of the wounded
sibling on two occasions in a hundred to being there on one in three.
Care arrived whole. And it taught its limit: the gift comes late,
because one enters danger when someone is hunting you, and with the
hunter on top there is no square where to leave anything the other
could pick up.

**Giving before, and giving from what you lack.** The answer was to
bring the gesture forward: if you carry no bandage and I do, I leave it
for you before you need it. That is the provision of section 3. Its
first version required the giver to carry two bandages, so as not to be
left with none; in the third series nothing happened, and the reason was
in the agent itself: it does not hoard. Carrying two bandages happened
in six instants of play in a thousand, because the row that makes the
empty bag hurt goes quiet with a single bandage inside. Provision could
only light up in a situation the agent itself almost never produced. It
was changed to what gives the rung its name: provision stops requiring
a surplus and is born with a single bandage of one's own and none of
the sibling's. What prevents the agent from giving away its only bandage
while it is itself at the edge of death is not a written threshold but
its own slope: in the image of the gesture of giving, the bag is left
empty, and that emptiness weighs all the more the less health the giver
has. Crossing one's own health with the sibling's yields a diagonal that
no one wrote: healthy and with the sibling worse off, I give; wounded,
I heal myself; the sibling healthy though without a bandage, I do not
waste the only one I have.

**Food and the bandage.** Nine in ten gifts of the clean series were
rations, by the asymmetry that section 3 left declared. A ration heals
less, but it heals under fire, which is where the sibling usually is
when it needs it; and a ration of fifteen heals less than a strong rival's sword blow takes: it covers one more blow, not a way out. That is why the gift of a ration is care, but
not rescue. The two scenes that follow tell of bandages, and that is why
they are the exception and not the rule.

**Eight bandages.** Of the eight bandage gifts in the clean series, six
were given by a clear margin, moved by the row of the wounded: one
sibling with full or nearly full health, the other in critical at one
or two squares, and the bandage goes out without a dilemma. The only two
ties are the two cases in which the row of the wounded was mute, because
the report the giver had said sixty-seven and was old. The one that gave
without hesitating had a fresh number for the sibling's health. The two
that gave on a tie only had the health band that is seen from outside,
and the tie was broken by a rule of the decider, not by the other's
need. It is the lesson of 4.1 seen from the other side: when what is
known arrives fresh, the row converts it, and the gift goes out with a
margin. There was also one case in which healing and giving competed in
earnest. The giver was wounded and carried a single bandage; using it on itself and leaving it for the sibling both carried real weight in the decision. It did not see the sibling: what it had was a report from a
second and a half before that put it at forty-four and without
bandages. It dropped the bandage, and it was not a tie: giving won
clearly. It is the case that sustains "down to its last bandage". And a
limit that must be said with its figure: giving well is not
coordinating well. Of the eight bandages dropped, three were picked up
by the sibling, and all three while it was worse off than when they
were given; the other five stayed on the ground or were surplus. And
none of the three came to heal: all three were used almost at once, and
in all three a blow or death cut the channel before it ended. All three
died with the sibling's bandage in the bag. Of the eight scenes, the
only cure that was completed was that of the one that healed with its
own. The cause is not in a row: the report says what one carries, never
what one has left on the ground.

**A temptation that was not needed.** It was also tried that being far
from the sibling, in calm, be a background unease, so that they would
tend to walk together. It did not serve: below a certain size it
changed nothing, and from there up the only thing that changed was that
the agent stopped picking up the food it had two squares away in the
opposite direction. And it was not needed, because the ring already
brings them together: the fire that tightens from outside ends up
taking both to the same place. It was left built and switched off.

**Two scenes from the diary.** The diary of the pack is an account of
the games of the clean series written only with what the reports say —
position, health, poison, bandages, aggressor —, with no invented
dialogue and without guessing what the report leaves unsaid. To read
the figures: the two siblings are born with one hundred points of
health; below sixty the slope begins to weigh; a sword blow from a
strong rival takes almost twenty, and a blow kills when the health that
remains does not reach what it takes.

**What it taught.** The first scene is the limit, and it is also the
weakest of the eight bandage gifts of the series: a tie, with the cure
out of the vote through a fault of the agent's own — its healing clock
does not notice that a blow has cut it, and it bars itself from healing
for almost two seconds believing it is still healing —, and with two
bandages through another fault: dropping empties the slot. The two
faults are declared in section 6; they have not been corrected within
this work. What the scene truly teaches is that the gift comes late when
the hunter is on top, and that a gesture of care can be born at the
worst possible moment and be of no use. The second scene is care at a
distance as far as it goes: knowing without seeing, going, and leaving.
Picking up, no: the one that was worse off healed with the bandage it
already carried, and the one left for it stayed on the ground. And the
whole rung taught something about rows, without characters. The first
provision only lit up if the giver carried two bandages; it seemed
prudent, and nothing happened in forty games. The reason was not in the
row but in the rest of the agent: the row that makes the empty bag hurt
goes quiet as soon as there is one bandage inside, so the agent almost
never picked up a second. A new row does not act in a void; it acts on
the situations the other rows already create or prevent, and if those
situations do not occur, the new row does not occur. That is why it was
changed: provision came to light up with a single bandage of one's own,
when the sibling has none left. That is giving from what one also
lacks, and it is what gives the rung its name.

### 4.3 The pack with a name

**What was added.** The pack of section 3: whoever is hitting my sibling
presses on me too, if it is certain and if I carry a weapon that does
damage. Certain meant, at first, that the sibling's fresh report said it
was being hit and from what position, and that in that position there
was an enemy in sight. From then on two counts are kept, and stated
separately: strict initiations — hitting one that has attacked neither
of the two —, which remain zero always, and defences on the sibling's
behalf — the first blow against the other's certified aggressor.

**What happened, first series.** With that row in the table, the sibling
began to defend: twelve times in seven games a sibling put itself
within reach of the other's aggressor and returned the blow, and in one
game the two took turns against the same hunter, each after it had
struck the other. This work calls that behaviour a pack, and from it
comes its title. Here the pack is two, and the word is used in its
animal sense: a group whose members look after one another, warn one
another and defend one another to survive. Nothing in the row counts
how many they are: it looks at whoever a fresh report names as a
sibling's aggressor, and with three or four it would be the same rule
with more reports to read. That has not been tested; what has been
tested is the smallest possible pack. The eight of the hive were already
a pack in that sense, but without knowing who was who; the two here are
the first pack whose members know one another. And two things appeared that the
agent did not know how to do. The first is the example section 3 uses
for "futures, not actions": four blows from one sibling to the other in
the whole series, three of them with the spear, defending the other
from an aggressor standing right behind it. The agent, on imagining its
spear blow, did not include the sibling standing in between; as soon as
the image included it, the sibling's rows made that future hurt, and
the spear stopped being driven through it. The second: of twenty times
in which a sibling saw the other attacked, with certainty and with a
weapon, in twelve the blow was physically impossible because the hunter
was not within its reach, and the agent stayed still watching the fight
three squares away. Just as approaching the wounded relieves a little
before arriving, approaching until the sibling's aggressor is within
range came to relieve a little the pressure of seeing it attacked.
Neither of the two is a new row: they are two corrections of what the
agent imagines.

**What happened, second series.** With the two corrections, in the clean
series — the one that no longer had our agent alone seated among the
rivals (section 3) —, the rung's headline figure: in forty games, one
hundred and four defences of the sibling, zero strict initiations and
zero blows between siblings. The agent defends when it can and does not
wound the one it saves. What it costs it was measured on its health in
the ten seconds after defending: twenty points at the median, and not
one defender fallen. And then it was measured better: defending costs
no more than standing beside a hunted one. That it also protects the
hunted one goes in the right direction and is not shown.

**What was put into the report.** The aggressor's seat: the number of
the one hitting me, which the one being hit knows with certainty and no
one else can see. With the name in the report, the sibling no longer
looks for the hunter at a position from four seconds ago but where it
is now, and of the six times in which it had not defended before, in
three it now defended; the other three remained without a possible blow,
and rightly so. What rivals do when a sibling answers them was also
looked at: no fight ended with one of the two dead, and in almost
half the aggressor left after about four blows. With that picture in
view, the response to blows was left as it was.

**What it taught.** That the care of this agent knows how to strike
without striking first, and knows whom: the pack does not hunt on
rumours, and does not wound the one it saves.

### 4.4 Memory

**What was looked at first.** When a hunter leaves, does it leave? The
eighty games of the two series were gone through looking for returns —
a seat that had already harmed one of the two and that is in sight
again —, counting as aggressor only what the agent itself could know:
whoever hit it, or whoever the sibling's report named. The rung's
headline figure: one hundred and thirty-eight returns in eighty games,
almost twice as many as there were fights. The world is small; those
that hit rotate, leave and come back, with four and a half seconds at
the median between going and returning, and in more than half of the
returns the one coming back had hit the sibling and was now coming for
the other.

**The blow that was not written.** The exact case was looked at — it
returns, armed, it approaches — and fifteen came out. In seven, it
struck first. In six, no one struck anyone. A preventive blow would have
fallen, in four cases out of ten, on one that was not going to attack;
and in one hundred and thirty-eight returns there was a single death of
ours. It was not written. What was added was fear: that the agent be
frightened before the blow on seeing a known aggressor come armed, and
that what comes out of that fear — distance, wall, sibling, nothing — be
decided by the body.

**What was added, and what it did.** A memory per game of the seats that
have harmed either of the two, with the damage they did. What is
remembered is a seat, not an individual: the number is valid while the
game lasts, and when it ends, that seat is occupied by another and
nothing remains. And a row that lights up when one of them is in sight,
armed and approaching, with the size of the fear that damage would
cause. It was tested over the fifteen real situations. The row lit up
when it should — thirteen of fifteen —, seven clock steps before the
blow; it lit up even before one that had never touched it, because the
sibling had told it the seat. And it did not change a single decision.
Why, in plain words: the agent does not decide row by row. Every future
it imagines carries on top of it, summed, everything that already hurts
it at that moment — the health it is missing, the hunter pressed
against it, the ring closing in —, and in a return those three things
are usually pushing hard. A new fear the size of a blow, some eighteen
points, is small beside them: it was added, but it did not change the
order of the futures; the one that won before went on winning. That is
why a row can light up when it should — thirteen of fifteen, seven
steps before — and not move a single decision, and that was known before
playing it in the field.

**Remembering what it cost.** The first memory kept of each aggressor
only the last thing it had done: one blow, some eighteen points. On
looking at the fifteen returns, those coming back were not one-blow
types: between the two siblings they had taken fifty-two points at the
median in the game, and one of them more than two hundred — two whole
lives. The fear that comes from remembering a blow is small; the one
that comes from remembering everything that seat has done in the game is
real. The memory was changed to sum all the damage of that aggressor to
either of the two, and it was tested again over the same fifteen
situations. Fear came to move the action in eight of the fifteen, and
all eight were the same thing: to stop standing still and gain
distance. Without a single blow born of memory, and without a single
instant in which fear made it abandon a cure or a meal. That version is
the one that closes the work.

**What it taught, and what it did not.** It is a result over fifteen real
situations, checked by making the agent decide over them; it was not
played in the field, because with seven cases per series of forty no
direction could have been seen. It is said so and left so. What it
teaches is that this agent can remember whoever did it harm without that
turning it into a hunter: the memory serves it to move away, not to
strike.

**The ladder, seen whole.** Seen from afar, the ladder is a single story.
First the agent was given a voice, and the voice was of no use, because
there was nothing in it that was hurt by what the other said. Then it
was given that pain and that relief — the sibling's health as its own
health —, and care appeared: first up close, then at a distance, then
giving what one carries before it is needed. Then it was told who was
hitting the sibling, and care learned to defend without hunting. At the
end it was given memory, and the fear that came out of it made it move
away, not strike. On no rung was a behaviour written for it; on each
one it was given something that hurt or relieved, and the behaviour came
out on its own, or did not. At the end of the ladder, in the scenes of
the one that goes alone — where there is no sibling in the game, neither
in sight nor by radio —, the agent goes on deciding exactly the same as
the one from the previous season, checked decision by decision.
Everything it does anew — defending, giving, moving away from the one
that returns — it does only when the other is in the scene. The whole
difference between the agent alone and the agent in a pair is the other.

## 5. The proof by absence

The previous section told what appeared when a few rows that looked at
the sibling were added to the agent. This one tells what disappears
when they are taken away. It is the other half of the same test, and
the one that answers the most serious objection that can be made to
this work. The headline figure is a pair of numbers: zero defences of
the sibling in forty games without the rows, one hundred and four with
them.

### 5.1 The objection

To everything this work has told, one objection can be made, and it is
worth stating in plain words, because it is the most serious it has. The
agent does many things: it eats, it heals, it moves away, it responds to
whoever hits it. How much of that is the engine, the machine that
imagines the next state and chooses the one that hurts least, and how
much is the table written by hand for this world? If the table does all
the work, the engine is an ornament, and what is called care here would
be only a well-disguised list of instructions. The objection weighs more
here than anywhere else, because the ladder of section 4 added new
rows, and every new row is one more occasion to say: the behaviour was
written there.

There is only one way to answer with data, and it is the one the design
allows: to remove the rows and look at what goes with them. If care
disappears when the rows that look at the sibling are switched off, and
everything else stays the same, then those rows contribute exactly
care, no more and no less. If things disappear that should not — if the
agent without social rows struck earlier, or struck the sibling —, then
the rows would be doing more than they say. And if care does not
disappear, the whole argument would be wrong. The test was done at two
levels: over recorded scenes, and in the field.

### 5.2 Over the recorded scenes

The first thing was to make sure the rows were separable: that each one
changes the value of a future only when the sibling is in it, and that
in the scenes of the one that goes alone — where there is no sibling in
the game, neither in sight nor by radio — the agent of the ladder
decides exactly what the one that goes alone decided. That was checked
at each rung, before playing it in the field, by making both versions
decide over the same recorded scenes: in none of the three versions that
climbed the ladder was there a single decision different from the
previous one when the sibling was not in the scene. That is the weak
form of the claim. It says that the new rows rewrote nothing of the one
that goes alone; it does not say what they contribute when the sibling
is there.

For the strong test a bare version was needed: the agent as it was at
the end of the previous work, without any of the ladder's rows, seated
as a pair in the same world. Before spending a single game it was
checked, state by state over six scenes — the agent at full health, at
half health and nearly dead, each with the sibling in front of it and
without —, that this bare version decided exactly the same as the
historical one, and that with the wounded sibling in front of it no
social row lit up. It was also checked that, with the ladder's rows
switched off, the agent of section 4 passed one by one the twelve tests
of the one that goes alone. With that, what was going to be measured in
the field was one thing only: the absence of the rows.

### 5.3 In the field: the same series, without the rows

Two copies of the bare version played forty games as a pair against the
same fourteen rivals, seat by seat, that the pair of section 4 had faced
in the series of the third rung, the one with the one hundred and four
defences. Before playing it in the field, six things were left written
that had to happen if the argument was true: that there would be no
defence of the sibling; that there would be no gift next to it; that the
agent's responses to whoever hits it would stay within three in ten of
the series with rows, and that it would go on not striking first; that
the distance between the two siblings would be greater; that there
would be neither a blow nor a chosen attack against the sibling; and
about the score no sign was written, because there was no reason to
expect one. The following table gives the result, measured with the
same criteria in both series.

The left-hand column is the pair without the rows that speak of the
sibling; the right-hand one, the same pair with them. Same fourteen
rivals, the same number of games — not the same games —, same counting
criteria. *Gift* is here an instant in which one of the two chooses to
drop an object — a bandage or a ration — with the other alive and within
two squares. It is a wider criterion than that of the diary of section
4, which counted only bandages; it was applied equally to both series so
that the comparison would be fair, and that is why the figures do not
match those given there. Five of the six things written down happened.
The sixth did not: there was a gift, one only, and that gift is the
subject of the next subsection. The games of one series and of the
other are not paired one to one, and that limits what can be claimed
about the small differences; about the large ones, it does not.

### 5.4 The gift that was not supposed to be there

The story this work was going to tell was clean: a body without social
rows against a body with them. A single gift in forty games was enough
to bring it down, and it deserves to be told in full, because it
corrects something true.

It happened eighty-three seconds into a game. One of the two, with the
sibling a little over one square away and at sixty health, chose to
drop its rations. In the bare version there was no row from the ladder;
what lit up was one of the two rows from the previous work that already
looked at the partner, the one of the small unease over its harm seen
up close, which that day was at half its range. That row, and the
other, the grief over the partner's death, had never been switched off
because they had never been counted as social. They were, in the agent
of the previous season, the smallest possible version of care: a pain
of one's own over another's harm, with no voice to say how much it was
or whom it came from.

The honest comparison is not, therefore, without the social against
with the social. It is *minimal* social against *complete* social, and
that is how it is stated from here on: from one ration to ninety-eight
gifts, and from zero to one hundred and four defences. The correction
costs the argument nothing and sharpens it. What the ladder's rows add
is not that care exists, which already existed in embryo, but its reach
— the sibling's health read from the report, at a distance, instead of
a neighbour's harm seen up close —, its cost — the last bandage — and
its direction — the one that is hitting it.

### 5.5 Whether caring costs points

There remains the question this world makes louder than any other:
whether caring for the sibling costs the agent points. No prediction was
written, because the question was truly double. Caring could cost it
dearly: the one that goes towards the sibling does not go towards a weapon
or towards a weak rival, and loses time and chances. Or it could give it
an advantage: two that heal each other last longer, and lasting is what
scores.

The result over forty games is this. The bare pair scored somewhat more
than the complete one, a little over three points per game against a
little over two and a half, and finished in the same place: the same
median position in both series, and the two pairs falling together,
both in the tail places, the same number of games. With forty unpaired
games, that difference is below what the test can tell from chance; the
detail is in the appendix.

The exact sentence the data allows, and no other, is this: caring for
the sibling gave it no advantage on the scoreboard, and it is not shown
that it cost it any. It matters to say it in full, without overstating
it: in a world that pays for killing, what this agent does for its sibling it does not do for the scoreboard, which does not reach it. It does it because of what hurts it.

### 5.6 What the rows contribute, and what they do not

Three things follow from the table. The first is that defence and the
gift belong to the social rows: without them there is not a single
defence in forty games, and there is one gift, that of the small row.
The second is that restraint does not. The bare pair strikes first
exactly as many times as the complete one — none — and does not touch
the sibling either, and that cannot come from rows it does not carry.
It comes from what the agent alone already carried: the row that
responds to whoever hits it, which is relieved by hitting that one and
only that one, and the fact, told in section 3, that a blow of its own relieves only the pressure of an aggressor that is already hitting it — its imagined blow lowers that aggressor's foreseen health, and no row makes anyone else's foreseen health worth striking —, so that there is no appetite in it to attack anyone, sibling or not. What the ladder's rows
add is not restraint, which was already there; it is whom to defend.

The third is that the social rows do not make the agent fight less for
itself. With them lit, its responses to whoever hits it rise by a little
under a third in the raw count, from seven hundred and eleven to nine
hundred and eighteen, and by a little over a tenth per recorded tick —
the log of the series without the rows covers less, as section 6 says —,
within the margin left written beforehand and with no predicted
direction. The simplest reading is that the two are closer to each other
— the median distance drops from four squares and a quarter to a little
over three and a half — and so are more often within the reach of
whoever strikes; but with forty unpaired games that is a reading, not a
measure.

It is worth saying where this leaves this work with respect to what has
already been done. That an agent attend to the other's situation has
been achieved before by putting it into the reward — the inequity
aversion of Hughes et al. (2018) is the clean case: the comparison with
the other enters the number the agent maximises —, and also by choosing
altruism as the objective of optimisation without external reward
(Franzmeyer et al. 2022). Here it is in neither of the two places: it is
in what hurts it, and it does not touch the world's scoreboard. That is
why switching off a row and looking at what disappears is, in this
set-up, a question with a direct answer.

With this, the objection of section 5.1 has an answer with a size, and
it is worth saying how far it reaches. The rows written by hand for this
world are what care depends on: without them there is no defence, one gift, and less closeness. They are not what restraint depends on, which was
already in the one that goes alone — in its earlier table and in what it
imagines —; what part of that belongs to the engine and what part to the
old table has not been separated, and section 6 leaves it as the test
that is missing. In the score and in survival no difference was seen
that forty games can tell apart. And that those rows contribute care
without saying what to do is not shown by removing them: it is shown by
their form, which anyone can read, and that is the limit section 6
declares. What remains when they are removed is an agent that goes on
not striking first, and that does not turn towards its sibling when it
hears it fall.

## 6. The limits

This work has the limits of what it is: forty games per series, a single
world, the same fourteen rivals, and an agent whose table its author
wrote by hand. They are all stated here, in four groups: what the
evidence can see and what it cannot; what is written by hand and what
that means; what the agent does not know or see; and two faults of the
agent that played.

### 6.1 What forty games can see

Each series is forty games of about three minutes, and forty games see
only the large effects. Zero defences against one hundred and four can
be seen; half a point of difference on the scoreboard cannot. That is
why there are results that are stated with their size and not as
demonstrated. That defending costs no more than standing beside is,
strictly, that no difference was seen in thirty-two pairs of
situations; with forty games that does not show that there is none.
That the defended sibling comes out better goes in the right direction,
and is not shown. And memory — the fear of the one that returns — was
never played in the field: it is a result over fifteen real situations,
checked by making the agent decide over them, because with seven cases
per series it could not have been seen any other way. What this work
claims about memory is that, and no more.

Nor are the series paired. The series with the rows and the series
without them had the same rivals and the same number of games, but not
the same games, and the small differences between the two mean nothing.
There is a single world, a single set of rivals and a single body.
Section 3 showed that changing who sits changes the world; what has
been measured here holds for these fourteen, and of another fourteen
nothing is known.

There is, besides, a gap in the logs themselves, said in section 3: the
logs of the agents that live long do not begin at the first tick,
because the platform cuts them when they reach a fixed size and goes on
from zero. There is a log for six of every ten ticks the pair lived in
the series with the rows and for a little over five in the series
without them, and what is missing is the beginning of the long games,
which are the ones that went best. That changes how two kinds of number
are read. The counts — one hundred and four defences, ninety-eight
gifts, one hundred and thirty-eight returns — are counts over what
was recorded, and in the whole games they can only be larger. The
absences — never struck first, no blow between siblings — hold over what
was recorded, and of the lost stretches nothing is known: in some of
them the agent did hit someone, the world's counter says so, and the log
that would say whether it was answering or starting is not there. That
is why the comparison of responses between the series with the rows and
the series without them is made per recorded tick, and gives a little
over a tenth, in the same direction as the raw count. Whether the whole
log remains on the server has not been checked, because checking it
means downloading again, and this work downloads nothing after closing
its series.

It is also worth saying what kind of evidence this is. The first work
of the series measured the agent from inside — distances, forces —;
this one measures it by what it does in the field, read from the logs.
They are two different kinds of evidence and they are not mixed. And
what was measured here is cooperation at a cost to oneself, and only
that: defending cost health in the ten seconds that followed — twenty
points at the median, though standing beside without defending cost
much the same —, and giving cost the last bandage. That is what this
work calls care; nothing more generous was measured.

Lastly, non-violence was possible only rarely, and it is worth saying clearly
what that means. The agent does not kill by decision or by rule: there
is nothing in it that killing serves, so the question is not whether it
managed to restrain itself, but how far one that never attacks gets in a
world where kills score points. The answer, with figures. The agent
alone, before there were two, was the last one standing five times out
of two hundred and forty in our own series, two of them without killing
anyone, and four out of seventy-two in the public league, one without
killing. As a pair, the two siblings fall into the tail places in eleven
out of every forty games. Surviving to the end without killing happens,
and it happens rarely: the normal thing, for an agent that does not
attack, is to die before the game ends. That is what not killing costs
in this world. The work does not say that non-violence wins; it says
that one can live that way, and how much it costs.

### 6.2 What is written by hand

The table was designed by the author, and this is said plainly. Its
sixteen rows and its numbers came from two hands, and each number
carries the mark of which: design decisions taken before playing, and
adjustments made during the work by looking at recorded scenes of real
games — trying a value, seeing what the agent did, correcting. That is,
put crudely, a calibration with a human as optimiser and a very small
sample. There was no automatic training of any kind, but a human eye
adjusting over scenes; the field series were played afterwards, and
that eye had not seen them.

It is worth adding that writing that table by hand is not a shortcut of
this work, but the ordinary practice of the field for decades: the
cognitive structure of emotions of Ortony, Clore and Collins (1988),
which is the most cited reference in the area, is also a table written
by hand. The difference from the usual appraisal architectures lies
elsewhere. A model like EMA (Marsella and Gratch 2009) derives the
appraisal from a causal interpretation of the situation: what has
happened, who caused it, whether it could be avoided. This table derives
nothing: it declares. And the cut that defines this work is best seen
stated against Frijda (1986), for whom an emotion already includes the
tendency to act in a particular way. Here the cut is made just before
that: what hurts and what relieves is declared, and what is done with
it is written nowhere. That frontier is the contribution and also the
limit: if someone found in the table a row that, instead of saying how
much something hurts, said what to do, everything this work claims about
behaviour would fall apart. That is why section 5 removes rows and looks at what goes with them, and leaves the form of the rows for anyone to read.

There remains an objection of substance that this work cannot settle.
If emotions have no fixed signature — if they are constructions that
vary with situation, culture and person (Barrett 2017) —, a fixed table
of sixteen lines is suspect by definition. The answer that can be given
is that the table neither names nor categorises emotions: it declares
what hurts and what relieves along bodily axes, which is where that
same theory places the regulation of the body. A reader coming from the
opposite tradition, that of basic emotions with a signature of their
own (Ekman 1992), will read the table differently. This work does not
settle between the two, and not out of caution but because it uses the
word for something else. Here an emotion is neither a category with a
fixed signature nor a construction that varies with culture: it is a
position in a space of three axes — a combination of how far the body,
the resources and the other are displaced —, and its size is the number
that moves the agent. The names — fear, care, bond — are regions of
that space that the reader may call whatever they like; the table does
not name them. It is a concept distinct from that of the two traditions,
and it is declared as such.

"No scripted behaviour" is, for that reason, a truth of degree, and it
is said with its degree every time it is said. What is written is what
hurts and what relieves, how much and on which axis; what is not
written is the step from feeling to doing. The full formula this work
holds is this: a homeostatic appraisal inside an architecture with
memory and explicit rules — the report, the aggressor's seat and the
memory of the game are written mechanisms —, where what each part
contributes is measured by removing it. And "without reward" means
exactly without training and without using the scoreboard. The agent
does have a single criterion, the distance to its equilibrium, which is
a number, and it decides by approaching it; it is not claimed that it
decides without a criterion, but that that criterion is not the world's
scoreboard and was not learned from it.

That distinction has an objection with a name, and it is better to state
it than to wait for it. Keramati and Gutkin (2014) showed that
maximising a reward and defending homeostasis are mathematically
equivalent objectives; if that is so, calling this "without reward"
would be a play on words. The equivalence they show holds for an agent
that learns a policy by maximising that number over time: there is a
value function, there are temporal differences, there is credit
assigned between what was done before and what came out afterwards.
This agent has none of that. There is no value function, no policy that
improves with experience and no credit to assign: at every instant it
imagines the states it can reach and chooses the one that hurts it
least. What is claimed, then, is not that there is no number — that is
already conceded —, but that that number was not learned and is not
aligned with the world's scoreboard.

There is a second form of the same objection. The published rebuttal of
the claim that reward is enough to explain intelligence (Silver et al.
2021) proposes a vector of rewards instead of a single one (Vamplew et
al. 2022), and a reader may ask how three homeostatic axes differ from
that under another name. In two sentences: the axes are not maximised
nor summed with learned weights, and there is nothing that grows when
things go well for the agent. They define a distance to an equilibrium,
and the only thing the agent does with it is descend it.

There remains an asymmetry declared in section 3 and unresolved. The
row of provision lights up when the sibling lacks bandages, is relieved
by giving any cure, and what comes out of the bag is the first thing
there, almost always a ration. It has not been decided whether that is a
scarcity of bandages or an agent that drops the first thing it finds;
telling them apart requires counting the bag at every gift, and it was
not done.

And there is a test missing that this work did not do and that would be
the next one: that of the engine. Section 5 removed rows and measured
what each contributes; no one has yet changed the appraisal itself — the
geometry of value, the way of summing pains and choosing the future that
hurts least — leaving the table and the decider as they are. Until that
is done, what section 5 attributes to the engine is attributed by
elimination, not by direct test.

It is worth saying why that hand was necessary. This agent does not
learn: nothing in it adjusts with experience how much a thing hurts it
or relieves it. An animal extracts that logic on its own, by living;
here a human extracted it from outside, by looking at recorded scenes
and correcting. What was written by hand is not the behaviour, but the
translation of this world into the agent's language: which things in
ZERO-SUM hurt and which relieve, how much and on which axis. That this
translation is done by a person is the limit. The ideal would be for
the agent itself to extract it, adjusting game by game how much each
thing in this world hurts it and how much it relieves it; today that is
far off, and it is the first piece this work would put in place next.

### 6.3 What the agent does not know or see

Three things about the agent's body limit what its results mean.

The first: the agent does not imagine the effect of a blow on itself. What its own blow takes, it knows, and section 3 says what it does with it; but it does not know that at sixteen health a strong rival's sword blow kills it and an
arrow does not, nor that at twelve either of the two kills it. Its fear
near death comes from the slope of its own health and from having
someone on top of it hitting it, and it is the same with a sword in
front of it as with a knife. The damage of each weapon is fixed and
known, so it is an open door: it could be given. It is left as a door
and not as a result, because nothing this work claims rests on the
agent knowing it: the defences, the gifts and the withdrawals happened
without that information. Giving it to it would change what it does
near death, and that would be another result.

The second: memory is of a position, not of an individual. What the
agent remembers of whoever did it harm is a seat number, valid while the
game lasts; when the game ends, that seat is occupied by anyone else and
nothing remains. No reputation is possible across games, because the
neighbour does not repeat; and the sibling is no exception: it is a seat
number that arrives once, at the start. Everything this work calls
recognising, remembering and defending happens within three minutes and
is erased with them. It is worth saying what kind of recognition that
is, because it has a name. The agent does not recognise an individual:
it recognises "the seat that did us harm in this game", which is a
temporary label on a category — aggressor — and is erased when the game
ends. Many species do something similar: they respond to "a predator" or
to "a neighbour that behaved badly recently" without keeping a memory
of individuals across a lifetime (Tibbetts and Dale 2007). It is a real
form of recognition, with its properties and its ceilings, and this
agent is in that box and not in the one next to it. Within the game
there are also two gaps, stated in section 3: poison arrives without an
author, and the camouflaged one disappears from the list of the visible;
in those two cases there is no seat to fear.

The third: giving well is not coordinating well. Of the eight bandages a
sibling dropped next to the other, three were picked up, all three by
the one that was worse off; the other five stayed on the ground or were
surplus, and none of the three healed anyone: a two-second channel does
not hold under fire, and under fire is where the sibling is when it
needs it. The report says what one carries and how much health one has
left; it does not say what one has left on the ground, and the one that
could pick it up does not know it is there unless it sees it. The gift
relieves the giver whether or not the other needs it; in people that
has a name — impure altruism, or "warm glow" (Andreoni 1990) — and it is
one of the known reasons for over-giving. The care of this agent reaches
as far as dropping, and sometimes as far as the other's hand; as far as
its life, in these eight, not once. That the other makes use of it is
another matter, and it is not resolved.

### 6.4 Two faults of the agent that played

The agent that played the series of this work carried two faults that
were discovered on reading a scene — that of the two bandages, in
section 4 — and that are detailed here. The first is in the healing
clock: when a blow interrupts a bandage, the agent does not notice that
the cure has been cut, and for almost two seconds it cannot try again,
even as its health keeps dropping. The second is in the gesture of
dropping: the agent values dropping one bandage, but what it drops is
everything it carries in that slot of the bag, and if it carried two,
both come out. The two faults affect the same thing, cures and gifts
under attack, and push in the same direction: less healing of oneself,
and gifts larger than the agent valued. Neither of the two has been
corrected within this work: doing so would oblige replaying the series.
All the numbers given here are of the agent with the faults inside, and
they are left so.

These are the limits. None has been taken to the appendix so that it is
not seen, and none, stated in full, leaves without support the sentence
that opens the work: in what the logs keep of forty games against
these fourteen rivals, this agent never struck first, defended the
sibling one hundred and four times, gave it what it carried, and none of
that gave it an advantage on the scoreboard, as far as forty games can
see. What the sentence claims fits within what has been measured; what
does not fit has been said here.

## 7. What this means

### 7.1 What this says, and what it does not

The question at the start was whether a machine can move through a
violent world without being violent: live, care for another, and
sometimes get far. The answer has a size, and the size is part of the
answer. An agent that never strikes first was the last one standing
five times out of two hundred and forty games in our own series, and
four out of seventy-two in the public league. One can live that way.
One can live little.

It is worth saying carefully what kind of thing that is, because the
word that first comes to mind is the wrong one. This is not a virtue.
There is no temptation to fight against: there is nothing in the agent
that killing serves, no pressure that a blow to someone else relieves,
no future in which a stranger's death is better than its life. The
agent does not restrain itself, because it has no need to. What the
result shows is smaller than a virtue, and more useful: that the
violence of a world and the appetite of the one that inhabits it are
separable things. A world where kills score points does not oblige
anyone to want to kill in order to last in it. That is all that has been
shown here, and it is not little: in a world like that, the natural
thing is to expect the opposite.

Whether that says something about human societies — how much of the
violence in a society comes from what its members want and how much
from what gives an advantage in it — is a question that accompanies
this work and that this work does not answer. What it does let one see
is why in us it is so hard to look at and here so easy. In a society the
two things can also be read, but they are not in a single place: what
hurts is written in each one, and also in the world, which would teach
what hurts even if there were no one; what gives an advantage is in the
laws, in the prices, in what some tell others. They are three books —
oneself, the world, the others — and reading all three at once is hard.
In this agent the three are reduced to the minimum and kept apart:
sixteen lines for what hurts, the rules of a league for what scores
points, and a report by radio for what happens to the other. That is
why here they can be looked at separately, and in us they cannot.

### 7.2 Three echoes

What follows are three parallels that help to read what happened. They
are not proof of anything: none of them rests on a measurement of this
work, and no measurement of this work rests on them. They are stated
because a reader coming from psychology or from ethology will hear
them, and it is better to name them than to leave the reader to place
them alone.

**Knowing without feeling.** The patients with damage to the
ventromedial cortex described by Bechara and colleagues know perfectly
well which is the good option — they explain it, reason it, defend it —
and cannot manage to decide (Bechara et al. 1994); in the card task,
moreover, healthy subjects begin to avoid the bad decks before they can
say why (Bechara et al. 1997), and from there came the somatic marker
hypothesis (Damasio 1996). It is worth saying in the same sentence that
that hypothesis is disputed: it has been objected that the evidence does
not force its reading and that "deciding before knowing" can be
explained without it (Maia and McClelland 2004; Dunn, Dalgleish and
Lawrence 2006). The parallel that matters here does not depend on who
is right in that dispute, because it is simpler: the information arrives
intact at a system that no longer converts it into anything. The first
rung of this work is that case, made on purpose and without knowing it:
the agent with the report switched on, hearing its sibling's exact
health, and a table that had nowhere to put it. Of eight decisions, zero
changes. The lesson of the rung is that of the patient: knowing changes
nothing if nothing converts what is known into pain or into relief.

**The precision of help.** That an animal helps another without prize
and without training is documented: the rats of Ben-Ami Bartal, Decety
and Mason (2011) open the door of a trapped companion and afterwards
share the food. What the eight bandages of this work add to that
picture is not the help, but its aim. In chimpanzees, help hits the mark
— choosing the tool the other needs — only when the helper can see what
situation the other is in; without seeing it, it helps unspecifically
(Yamamoto, Humle and Tanaka 2012). And in people, knowing exactly what
is happening to the other is not enough for help to come out: wanting
to help is needed as well (Winczewski, Bowen and Collins 2016), on top
of what Ickes (1993) called empathic accuracy. Helping by hearsay is
helping worse. The eight bandages of section 4 can be read that way. Of
the sibling's health, two different things reach the agent: an exact
number, which travels by voice and can grow old, and a health band,
coarse, the one seen from outside as long as the other is in sight. In
six of the eight gifts the number was recent and said that the sibling
had very little health left; there the future in which it dropped the
bandage beat the others by a clear difference. The other two are exactly
the two cases in which the number it held was old — it said sixty-seven,
it did not say danger —, the row that responds to the wounded sibling
stayed quiet, and dropping tied with staying still: the tie was broken
by a rule that, faced with two equal futures, prefers doing to not
doing, and not by the other's need. Put short: the one that gave with a
margin had a number; the one that gave on a tie had only seen a health
band.

**The fear that moves away.** In many animals, the first response to a
known danger is not fighting but avoidance (Preston and de Waal 2002);
and the intensity of that response is calibrated not by the last scare,
but by the risk accumulated over time — it is the predation risk
allocation hypothesis (Lima and Bednekoff 1999). Between the last two
rungs the same thing happened. With the memory of the last blow, fear
lit up in time and did not move a single decision; with the full count
of each one's damage, it moved eight of fifteen, and all eight were the
same thing: to stop standing still and gain distance. None went towards
the blow.

### 7.3 Instrument and expression

Section 1 left posed a distinction between two ways in which a signal
can come out of an agent, and here it is closed. One is that of an agent
trained to maximise a scoreboard: if something resembling communication
appears in it, that signal is an instrument of the scoreboard, and it
exists as long as it scores points. The other is that of this agent.
Its report cannot be that, because the world's scoreboard does not reach
it by any path: it comes out because there is a body with a state and a
thread that transmits it, and if warning were of no use to it at all it
would go on warning just the same.

And it is worth saying what that distinction does not say, because it is
easy to hear more in it. It does not say what is inside one agent or
another; it does not measure them, rank them or judge them. It says
where behaviour comes from in each architecture — from a prize, or from
a state —, and that is a property of the design that is checked by
reading the code, not an opinion about anyone's interior. This work
describes how its agent is built. Of the other agents it also describes
how they are built, as far as is known, and nothing more: it does not
say what is inside any of them — neither its own nor the others.

### 7.4 Where it would go next

Four doors, named and not opened.

The first is announced at the end of section 6: that the logic of what
hurts and what relieves in a world be extracted by the agent itself, by
living in it, instead of by a person watching from outside.

The second is the test that section 5 is missing: to change the
appraisal itself, the way of summing what hurts and choosing the future
that hurts least, leaving the table and the decision as they are, to see
what remains of care when what changes is the engine.

The third is a world with a single life, where not killing truly costs
and not only a place in a table. Here, dying early is dropping a few
places; there it would be the end.

The fourth is the largest and the one that depends least on this work.
Almost all the worlds where agents can live today are made for language
agents, for reinforcement learning or for programmed behaviour: they are
asked for text, given reward, or told what they have to do. A
homeostatic creature lives in them on borrowed ground, and this series
has spent three works building bridges so that it fits. A purpose-built world can always be made, but a world of one's own is not a shared
world: no one can compare against you, and you cannot compete against
anyone. The door this work would leave open is an ecosystem of social
worlds — worlds with hunger, with harm, with others, with death — where
creatures made in another way can practise and be measured alongside
the rest.

### 7.5 The close

What moves this work, said for once in the first person, is a question
of alignment: whether machines will end up seeing humans as rivals or
as a group. The usual way of facing it is to write the values on the
outside: rules that forbid, preferences that are trained, lists of what
must not be done. This work looks elsewhere, and it is worth saying that
this is a wager of its author and not a result: that a creature's
values are not above its behaviour but beneath it, in what its body
counts as harm and as relief. The wager is not original: it is that of
morality grounded in the agent's physiology (Balkenius et al. 2016), and
in its recent formulation, that of values understood as an affective
orientation — approaching what relieves, moving away from what hurts —
and not as a preference someone specifies (Sennesh and Ramstead 2026).
What this work adds to that wager is not one more argument, but some
games played.

The three axes of this agent are that wager made small. Looking after
one's own body, looking after what is needed to stay alive, and looking
after the others and the place one occupies among them: on those three
things everything it does is decided. It is not claimed here that that is
all we call values in a person. Something smaller and checkable is
claimed: that with those three things, and without it being written
anywhere what to do with the others, an agent that lives where kills
score points cares.

And the world does the other half. The same three axes in a world where
no one can do harm would have produced nothing that deserved to be
called care: it took something hurting, and being able to hurt another.
If the wager is good, a value does not live only inside the one that
holds it: it lives between a body that feels and a world that gives
occasions for that to matter. From there comes what this work teaches,
which is minimal: an agent in which there is nothing that killing serves
does not need to be forbidden to kill. It is a door, not an answer.
